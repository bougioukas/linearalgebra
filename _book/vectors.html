2  Vector Spaces

In physical sciences, measurable quantities are generally classified into two fundamental types: scalars and vectors. A scalar is completely specified by its magnitude alone, that is, by a single numerical value, as in the case of mass or temperature. In contrast, a vector is characterized by both magnitude and direction, as in velocity and force. The mathematical framework that provides a rigorous description of vectors and the operations defined on them is the concept of a vector space, a fundamental structure in linear algebra.

2.1 Vectors in the Plane

A vector in the plane is represented geometrically by an arrow, i.e., a directed line segment whose initial point is the origin and whose terminal point is a point in the plane. The direction of the arrow indicates the direction of the vector and the length of the arrow represents its magnitude (Figure 2.1).

In this book, vectors are represented by lowercase letters set in boldface type, such as \(\mathbf{u}\). However, when we wish to specify that a vector \(\mathbf{u}\) has initial point A and terminal point B, we denote it by \(\mathbf{u} = \overrightarrow{AB}\).


From elementary physics, we know that two forces, \(\mathbf{F}_1\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2\), acting on the same object and separated by an angle \(\phi\), can be combined into a single resultant force given by the vector sum \(\mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2\). According to the parallelogram law of forces, this resultant is represented geometrically by the directed diagonal of the parallelogram constructed with \(\mathbf{F}_1\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2\) as adjacent sides (Figure 2.2 a).

We also know that if we multiply force \(\mathbf{F}_1\) by a scalar \(\lambda\), we obtain a force \(\lambda \cdot \mathbf{F}_1\) whose magnitude is scaled by \(|\lambda|\). If \(\lambda > 0\), the direction is preserved; if \(\lambda < 0\), the direction is reversed; and if \(\lambda = 0\), the result is the zero vector \(\mathbf{0}\) (Figure 2.2 b).


Vector addition and scalar multiplication, together with a specific set of properties, define the foundational framework of a vector space.

2.2 Definition of Vector Space

A non-empty set \(V\) is called a vector space over a field \(\mathbb{K}\) if its elements are equipped with a well-defined notion of equality, denoted by (\(=\)), and two closed operations: vector addition between elements of \(V\), denoted by (\(+\)), and scalar multiplication of a scalar in \(\mathbb{K}\) with an element of \(V\), denoted by (\(\cdot\)), satisfying the following axioms for all \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V\) and all \(\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}\):

Vector Addition Axioms

  1. Closure under Addition: The sum of \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\), denoted by \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\), is in \(V\).

  2. Additive Commutativity: \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\).

  3. Additive Associativity: \((\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})\).

  4. Additive Identity: There exists a unique vector \(\mathbf{0}\) in \(V\), called the zero vector, such that \(\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{u} =\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}\) for all \(u\) in \(V\).

  5. Additive Inverse: For each \(\mathbf{u}\) in \(V\), there is a vector \(-\mathbf{u}\) in \(V\) (called the opposite of \(\mathbf{u}\)) such that \(\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}\).

Scalar Multiplication Axioms

  1. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: The scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{u}\) by \(\lambda\), denoted by \(\lambda \cdot \mathbf{u}\), is in \(V\).

  2. Distributivity over Vector Addition: \(\lambda \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \lambda \cdot \mathbf{u} + \lambda \cdot \mathbf{v}\).

  3. Distributivity over Scalar Addition: \((\lambda + \mu)\cdot \mathbf{u} = \lambda\cdot \mathbf{u} + \mu\cdot \mathbf{u}\).

  4. Compatibility of Multiplication: \(\lambda \cdot (\mu \cdot \mathbf{u}) = (\lambda \cdot \mu) \cdot \mathbf{u}\).

  5. Scalar Identity Element: \(1 \cdot \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}\).

Additionally, for each \(\mathbf{u}\) in \(V\) and scalar \(\lambda \in \mathbb{K}\), the following properties holds as an immediate consequence of the vector space axioms:

\[0 \cdot \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\] \[\lambda \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\]


Unless stated otherwise, \(\mathbb{K}\) is taken to be \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), and \(V\) is referred to as a real or complex vector space, respectively.

NOTE: Axioms 1 through 5 state that \((V, +)\) forms an abelian (commutative) group. Axioms 6 through 10 define how the field \(\mathbb{K}\) acts on that group.

There exist many completely different types of vector spaces used throughout science and engineering, including:

  • Coordinate Spaces (\(\mathbb{R}^n\) and \(\mathbb{C}^n\)): Ordered n-tuples of real or complex numbers, ranging from the two- and three-dimensional spaces of classical physics to the high-dimensional arrays of modern data analysis.

\[\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_n + v_n \end{bmatrix} \text{,} \qquad \lambda \cdot \mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda \cdot u_1 \\ \lambda \cdot u_2 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda \cdot u_n \end{bmatrix}\]

  • Matrix Spaces (\(M_{m \times n}\)): The set of all \(m \times n\) matrices over a field \(\mathbb{K}\), where each matrix is treated as a single vector and addition and scalar multiplication are defined entry-wise,

\[\mathbf{U} + \mathbf{V} = [u_{ij} + v_{ij}] \text{,} \qquad \lambda \cdot \mathbf{U} = [\lambda \cdot u_{ij}]\]

allowing transformations, and systems of equations be represented and manipulated within a unified algebraic framework.

  • The Vector Space of Polynomials (\(\mathbb{P}_n\)): the set of all polynomials of degree at most \(n\) with coefficients from a field \(\mathbb{K}\) (typically \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\)), along with the zero polynomial. Vectors in this space are algebraic expressions, where addition and scalar multiplication are performed by combining coefficients of like powers of \(x\),

\[(p + q)(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} (p_i + q_i)x^i \text{,} \qquad (\lambda \cdot p)(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} (\lambda \cdot p_i)x^i\]

  • Function Spaces (\(C[a, b]\)): Continuous functions on a closed interval \([a,b]\), with addition and scaling defined pointwise,

\[(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x) \text{,} \qquad (\lambda \cdot f)(x) = \lambda \cdot (f(x))\]

forming infinite-dimensional spaces where whole continuous functions, mathematical curves, and physical waveforms behave exactly like vectors. This forms the foundation of signal processing, acoustics, and quantum mechanics.

2.3 Vector Subspaces

When analyzing a large vector space \(V\), we are frequently interested in smaller collections of vectors within \(V\) that behave as self-contained vector spaces in their own right. These collections are known as subspaces.

Formally, let \(V\) be a vector space over a field \(\mathbb{K}\), and let \(W\) be a subset of \(V\) (\(W \subseteq V\)). We say that \(W\) is a subspace of \(V\) if:

  1. It contains the zero vector: \(\mathbf{0} \in W\)

  2. It is closed under the inherited operation of addition: \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W \implies \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in W\)

  3. It is closed under the inherited operation of scalar multiplication: \(\mathbf{u} \in W, \lambda \in \mathbb{K} \implies \lambda\mathbf{u} \in W\)

Since the remaining axioms are inherited automatically from \(V\), these three conditions are both necessary and sufficient, and can be consolidated into the subspace test:

\[\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W, \ \lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K} \implies \lambda\mathbf{u} + \mu\mathbf{v} \in W\]


Example

In the Cartesian space \(\mathbb{R}^3\), every plane \(P\) passing through the origin can be written as

\[P = \{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid \alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z = 0\},\]

where \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in \mathbb{R}\) are not all zero. To verify that \(P\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), we check the three subspace properties.

1. Contains the zero vector

Substituting \((0,0,0)\) into the plane equation gives

\[\alpha(0) + \beta(0) + \gamma(0) = 0,\]

So the zero vector is in the plane.

2. Closed under addition

Let \(\mathbf{u} = (x_1, y_1, z_1) \in P\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (x_2, y_2, z_2) \in P\). Their sum is defined component-wise as:

\[\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (x_1 + x_2, \; y_1 + y_2, \; z_1 + z_2)\]

Since both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) lie in the plane \(P\), their coordinates must satisfy the plane’s defining equation:

\[\alpha x_1 + \beta y_1 + \gamma z_1 = 0\]

and

\[\alpha x_2 + \beta y_2 + \gamma z_2 = 0\]

Adding these two equations yields:

\[\alpha(x_1 + x_2) + \beta(y_1 + y_2) + \gamma(z_1 + z_2) = 0\]

Hence,

\[(x_1 + x_2, \; y_1 + y_2, \; z_1 + z_2) \in P\]

\[\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in P\]

3. Closed under scalar multiplication

Let \(\mathbf{u}(x_1, y_1, z_1) \in P\) and let \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\). Since

\[\alpha x_1 + \beta y_1 + \gamma z_1 = 0,\]

multiplying by \(\lambda\) gives

\[\alpha(\lambda x_1) + \beta(\lambda y_1) + \gamma(\lambda z_1) = 0\]

Therefore,\[(\lambda x_1, \lambda y_1, \lambda z_1) \in P\]

\[\lambda\mathbf{u} \in P\]

Since all three conditions are satisfied, every plane through the origin is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Moreover, the subspaces of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) are precisely:

  • The trivial subspace \(\{(0,0,0)\}\)
  • All lines through the origin
  • All planes through the origin
  • \(\mathbb{R}^3\) itself

2.4 Vector Space in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)

We can represent vectors as a sequence of entries (or elements) arranged in a column, called a column vector. For example, we write a vector \(\mathbf{u}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) as

\[\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{bmatrix}\]

Because writing vertically takes up a lot of space, this exact same vector is often written horizontally using parentheses and commas:

\[\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n)\]

 

Example: Vector \(u = (u_1, u_2)\)

For example, a vector \(\mathbf{u}\) in a two-dimensional space (\(\mathbb{R}^2\)) can be expressed algebraically as an ordered pair of its entries, \(u_1 = 1.5\) and \(u_2 = 1\), written as \(\mathbf{u} = (1.5, 1)\). Geometrically, it is represented as an arrow, or directed line segment, starting at the origin in the coordinate plane and ending at the point with coordinates \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) (Figure 2.3).

Implementation in R

We can initialize our specific vector \(\mathbf{u} = (1.5, 1)\) and print its entries to the console as follows:

u <- c(1.5, 1)
u
[1] 1.5 1.0

2.5 Vector Addition in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)

If \(\mathbf{a} = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n)\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n)\) are two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), then the sum of \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is the vector:\[\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_1 + b_1, a_2 + b_2, \dots, a_n + b_n)\]

We add two vectors, \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2)\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2)\), by adding their components:

\[u + v = (u_1, u_2) + (v_1, v_2) = (u_1 + v_1, u_2 + v_2)\]

 

Example: Vector addition u + v

\[w = u + v = (3, 1) + (1, 2) = (3 + 1, 1 + 2) = (4, 3)\]

Implementation in R
u <- c(3, 1)
v <- c(1, 2)

w <- u + v
w
[1] 4 3
